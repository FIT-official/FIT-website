# PLA vs PETG vs Marble and Wood PLA: Which Filament Should You Choose?

Which filament should you load for this job? It depends on what the part has to do. A display model, a bracket that sits near a window and a decorative vase all have different needs.

This guide compares four materials we sell at Fix It Today: plain PLA, PETG, Marble PLA and Wood PLA. All of them are 1.75mm filament on 1kg spools, and all of them work on most desktop FDM printers.

## Start with what the part must do

Before you think about colour, ask three questions:

1. **Where will the part live?** Indoors at room temperature, in a hot car, outdoors, or somewhere damp?
2. **Does it need to take a load or flex a little?** A clip or a hook behaves differently from a figurine.
3. **How should it look?** Glossy, matte, stone-like or wood-like?

Our [filament types guide](https://www.fixitoday.com/blog/3d-printing-filament-types-guide) puts it simply: choose the material from the part's environment and function, then pick the matching filament profile in your slicer before slicing.

## PLA: the best starting point

PLA is the material we recommend for most first prints. It is stiff, it prints cleanly and it holds small details well.

The main limitation is heat. PLA softens sooner than PETG, so it is not the right choice for parts that will sit in a hot car or near a heat source.

**Good for:** models, prototypes, enclosures for indoor use, jigs and fixtures that do not see heat, and classroom projects.

## PETG: for practical, functional parts

PETG (glycol-modified PET) is the material to reach for when a part needs to be tougher. Compared with PLA, it offers greater strength, durability and temperature and chemical resistance, while still being straightforward to print. That makes it a good fit for functional parts and parts that will be used outdoors or in damp places.

PETG has a couple of habits to know about. It can string more than PLA, and it can grip the build plate very strongly, so follow your printer maker's advice on plate surfaces and release. Slow down a little and tune retraction if you see fine hairs between parts.

**Good for:** clips, brackets, holders, covers, parts that see moisture, and anything that has to survive more handling than a display piece.

## Marble PLA: stone-like finish with no extra work

Marble PLA is PLA with a speckled, stone-like finish that comes straight off the printer. There is no painting or post-processing needed to get the effect. Because it prints like standard PLA, you can use your usual PLA settings as a starting point.

It suits pieces where looks matter: planters for indoor use, desk organisers, small sculptures, architectural models and gifts. It has the same heat limits as plain PLA.

## Wood PLA: warm, matte, and sandable

Wood PLA is PLA infused with real wood fibres. It gives a warm, matte, wood-grain finish that can be lightly sanded after printing. Like Marble PLA, it prints like standard PLA.

## What about ABS, ASA and TPU?

These are useful materials, but they are a step up. ABS and ASA need an enclosure and good ventilation because of warping and fumes, and ASA is the usual choice for parts exposed to sunlight and weather. TPU is flexible and prints more slowly. Our [filament types guide](https://www.fixitoday.com/blog/3d-printing-filament-types-guide) covers them in more detail.

## Keep your filament dry

Whatever you choose, damp filament causes problems: popping, stringing and weak layers. PETG in particular benefits from being kept dry. Store spools sealed with desiccant, and dry a spool before use if it has been left out.
